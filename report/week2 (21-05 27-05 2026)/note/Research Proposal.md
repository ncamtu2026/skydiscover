
## Title: Knowledge-Augmented AI-Driven Research for Systems
## Abstract
## Motivation
AI-Driven Research for Systems (ADRS) has emerged as a promising paradigm for automating algorithm discovery, demonstrating the ability to match or even surpass human state-of-the-art solutions across diverse systems problems. Its strength stems from two complementary forces. First, the iterative loop of generation, evaluation, and refinement — in which large language models act as the core generators that propose new solutions — progressively improves candidate solutions by feeding back execution results, scores, and prior attempts to guide each new generation. Second, these LLMs bring a breadth of knowledge from vast and diverse training datasets that transcends the specialized focus of individual human researchers, enabling the discovery of solutions that draw on patterns and techniques humans might overlook. Together, these forces allow ADRS to propose, refine, and discover solutions in ways that would take human researchers weeks or months to achieve.

Yet while existing frameworks do leverage information generated within this loop — through feedback, performance scores, and solution history — there remains significant untapped potential. Current approaches mostly reason about _individual_ solutions in the short term, with limited capacity to analyze the search process at a longer horizon and at a system level: when progress stalls, it is rarely clear whether the bottleneck lies in the search strategy, the evaluation signal, the prompting, or other components of the pipeline. Without such causal, long-term insight, the loop tends to fall back on extensive trial-and-error, consuming many iterations and substantial cost to make further progress. More advanced memory management and knowledge distillation techniques could exploit these accumulated signals more deeply, yet the field is still far from fully realizing what this internal stream of information can offer.

Looking further, ADRS frameworks also rely passively on whatever knowledge happens to be encoded in the LLM's parameters — a fixed snapshot frozen at training time. This creates two distinct limitations. On one hand, when problems involve recent developments, newly released systems, or highly specialized domains, the parametric knowledge may simply not contain what is needed, leaving the search with no path forward. On the other hand, even when relevant knowledge does exist within the model, the exploration of that knowledge remains largely uncontrolled. Although evolutionary mechanisms such as island populations and diversity-driven selection encourage the search to branch in different directions, _what_ is actually explored along each direction is still left to the LLM to surface on its own — making it a matter of chance whether the right idea emerges in time, and the loop can get stuck at locally optimal solutions when promising techniques that could break out are simply never invoked. A more principled approach, rather than waiting for the next generation of frontier models or hoping the LLM will eventually surface the right idea, is to actively augment the search with relevant external knowledge — enabling ADRS to draw on up-to-date literature, domain-specific resources, and cross-domain insights in a controlled and systematic way, and giving researchers a lever to inject promising directions instead of relying on serendipity.

Together, these gaps point to a broader open question: _how should knowledge — both internal and external, in-domain and cross-domain — be systematically integrated into ADRS to unlock solutions beyond the reach of parametric knowledge alone?_ Knowledge integration is by no means a guaranteed solution, but it represents a promising direction worth investigating. Addressing this question requires not only new methods for knowledge acquisition and injection, but also principled ways to evaluate whether a piece of knowledge is genuinely useful for the search.
## Related Work
### AI-Driven Research for Systems
The idea of using machines to automate scientific discovery has a long history, but recent advances in large language models have made it practically viable. FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024) established the foundational paradigm: LLMs act as mutation operators within an evolutionary loop, iteratively proposing and refining candidate programs guided by a programmatic evaluator. Building on this, AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025) generalized the approach to full codebases, introducing a MAP-Elites-based population structure and island model to maintain solution diversity, and demonstrated impressive results on GPU kernel optimization and algorithm discovery. A number of open-source frameworks followed, including OpenEvolve (Sharma, 2025) and CodeEvolve (Assumpção et al., 2025), democratizing access to LLM-driven evolutionary search.

Subsequent work has focused on improving how the search process exploits available information. GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2025) takes a step forward by evolving prompts rather than code directly, using full execution trajectories as context to diagnose failures and propose targeted prompt updates — introducing a form of causal reasoning at the solution level. ShinkaEvolve (Lange et al., 2025) introduces structured archive maintenance with meta-information, periodic meta-scratchpad summaries of successful strategies, and island-based knowledge diffusion across subpopulations. AdaEvolve (Cemri et al., 2026) treats the evolution process as hierarchical adaptive optimization, dynamically regulating the exploration-exploitation tradeoff based on fitness trajectory signals. EvoX (Liu et al., 2026) goes further by treating the search strategy itself as an evolvable object, maintaining a history of strategy performance to trigger adaptive switching when stagnation is detected.

Taken together, these frameworks represent a progression in how internal knowledge — the information generated within the search loop — is accumulated and reused. However, despite this progress, two fundamental gaps remain. First, while frameworks increasingly store richer information (execution traces, archive histories, strategy logs), they lack mechanisms for deep causal analysis: when progress stalls, it remains unclear which component of the pipeline — the search strategy, prompt design, evaluation signal, or solution generator — is the root cause. The accumulated signals are used reactively rather than being distilled into principled understanding of what drives success or failure. Second, and more fundamentally, all existing frameworks operate exclusively on internal knowledge. Every signal used to guide the search — feedback scores, solution histories, meta-summaries — is generated within the loop itself. No framework draws on external knowledge sources such as recent literature, domain-specific resources, or cross-domain techniques, leaving the search permanently bounded by what the LLM already knows at training time. These two gaps, and the absence of any mechanism to integrate internal and external knowledge in a unified way, motivate the knowledge-augmented framework we propose.
### Agentic Memory
A growing body of work has studied how LLM agents can retain and exploit information accumulated over time, drawing loose analogies to human memory systems where short-term working memory supports immediate reasoning and long-term memory enables recall across sessions.

Early work established the value of reflection and experience accumulation. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) introduced verbal self-feedback as a mechanism for agents to diagnose failures and refine behavior on subsequent attempts, but memory remains confined to a single task. ExpeL (Zhao et al., 2024) took a significant step further by operating across multiple tasks: the agent collects success and failure trajectories into an experience pool, extracts cross-task insights through comparative analysis, and retrieves relevant past experiences to guide future decisions. This paradigm of _distilling reusable knowledge from accumulated experience_ is directly relevant to ADRS, where insights about what works and what fails across many iterations could similarly be extracted and reused.

On the storage and retrieval side, Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) introduced a memory stream with principled retrieval based on recency, relevance, and importance — providing a structured way to surface the most useful memories rather than naively concatenating all history. MemoryBank (Zhong et al., 2024) further applies forgetting curve theory to model memory decay, encoding experiences as vector representations for similarity-based retrieval. MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023) draws an analogy to OS virtual memory, introducing a paging mechanism between an active context window and external long-term storage — a hierarchical design that prevents context overflow while preserving access to historical information. Mem0 (2025) extends this with a modular architecture that dynamically extracts and consolidates salient information, with a graph-based variant to capture relational structure among memory fragments.

More recent work has moved toward structured and self-organizing memory. A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025), inspired by the Zettelkasten method, dynamically constructs a network of interlinked knowledge notes that evolve as new information arrives. Dynamic Cheatsheet (Suzgun et al., 2025) introduces test-time learning with adaptive memory, where an agent iteratively builds and refines a structured summary of useful strategies during task execution — conceptually close to ShinkaEvolve's meta-scratchpad but with a more principled update mechanism. AgeMem (Yu et al., 2026) proposes a unified framework that jointly manages short-term memory (current context) and long-term memory (persistent knowledge store), arguing that effective agents require both high-quality long-term retrieval and efficient short-term context management.

These techniques collectively offer a rich toolkit for improving how internal knowledge is managed in ADRS: from principled retrieval strategies and hierarchical storage, to cross-iteration insight extraction and adaptive summarization. However, none of these works operate in the context of evolutionary search for systems optimization, and the specific challenges of ADRS — high-volume iterative execution, noisy evaluation signals, and the need to attribute progress (or stagnation) to specific pipeline components — remain unaddressed.
### Inspirational Retrieval
A parallel line of work has studied how LLMs can leverage external knowledge to generate novel scientific hypotheses — not by retrieving answers to questions, but by surfacing _inspirations_: knowledge fragments from seemingly unrelated domains that, when combined with a research background, can spark genuinely new ideas. This paradigm is grounded in a cognitive science observation that creative breakthroughs often arise from the association of two or more pieces of knowledge that were previously unconnected, a principle that has been shown to be discipline-independent (ResearchBench, 2025).

The foundational work in this space is Literature-Based Discovery (Swanson, 1986), which established that hidden knowledge can be uncovered by bridging concepts across disparate bodies of literature. Modern approaches have substantially extended this idea. SciMON (Wang et al., ACL 2024) was among the first to explicitly treat "inspiration" as a distinct type of knowledge — separate from background context — retrieving candidates from semantic neighborhoods, citation graphs, and knowledge graph neighbors, then iteratively refining generated ideas against prior work until sufficient novelty is achieved. MOOSE-Chem (Yang et al., ICLR 2025) formalized this into a three-stage pipeline: screen a large literature corpus for relevant inspiration papers, compose hypotheses by combining background and inspirations through an evolutionary algorithm, and rank the resulting hypotheses by novelty, validity, and significance. Notably, MOOSE-Chem demonstrated that LLMs with a 2023 training cutoff could rediscover chemistry hypotheses published in Nature and Science in 2024 — suggesting that LLMs already encode latent cross-domain associations not yet recognized by humans, and that structured retrieval can unlock them.

Subsequent work has explored richer retrieval mechanisms. ResearchAgent (Baek et al., NAACL 2025) augments retrieval with entity-centric knowledge graphs, mining shared underlying concepts across papers to bridge domains that text similarity alone would miss. KG-CoI (Xiong et al., 2024) grounds the entire generation process in structured knowledge graphs, using graph-based reasoning for both retrieval and hallucination detection — addressing a key weakness of flat RAG where rare but critical knowledge is often omitted. Chain of Ideas (Li et al., EMNLP 2024) organizes retrieved literature into a chain structure that mirrors the progressive development of a research domain, allowing LLMs to reason about the trajectory of ideas rather than treating retrieved papers as disconnected fragments.

A common challenge across all these approaches is _filtering_: given a large retrieval corpus, the vast majority of candidate inspirations will be irrelevant or redundant. Evaluating which retrieved knowledge is genuinely useful — along dimensions of novelty, relevance, actionability, and cross-domain transferability — remains an open problem, with surface-level similarity metrics shown to be insufficient for detecting deeper conceptual innovations.

These techniques collectively suggest a promising direction for ADRS: rather than relying passively on whatever cross-domain knowledge happens to be encoded in the LLM's parameters, the search loop could actively retrieve and inject inspirations from external literature at strategic points — surfacing techniques from adjacent domains that could guide the evolutionary search toward solutions it would otherwise never reach. Unlike existing inspiration retrieval work, which operates in a single-pass generation setting, applying this paradigm to ADRS introduces new challenges: _when_ during the search to trigger retrieval, _what_ to retrieve given the current state of the population, and _how_ to filter and integrate retrieved knowledge so that it genuinely augments rather than distracts the search.
